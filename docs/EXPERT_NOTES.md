# Notes on Grant Samuel's report

These are my notes on the independent expert's report in the Domain scheme booklet. Page numbers are the printed booklet numbers (the footer reads "Domain Group | Scheme Booklet 131"). Grant Samuel's own page numbers are the booklet number minus 102, so booklet p. 131 is its p. 29. The report runs from booklet p. 99 to p. 165, where the broker forecasts in Appendix 1 end it. I quote the report sparingly and give the page for every figure so each one can be checked against the booklet.

## The valuation summary, the methods and the conclusion (booklet pp. 103 to 137)

### What the deal was

CoStar agreed to buy every Domain share it did not already own by a scheme of arrangement, for A$4.43 cash per share, which includes a fully franked special dividend of up to 8.8 cents (p. 103). The sequence was an unsolicited proposal at A$4.20 on 21 February 2025, a revised proposal at A$4.43 on 27 March 2025 that CoStar called its best and final price, and a scheme implementation deed on 9 May 2025 (p. 103). CoStar had bought 16.96% of Domain at A$4.20 on 20 and 21 February 2025 (p. 103). Nine Entertainment held about 60% of Domain and said it intended to vote in favour (p. 104). The booklet gives Nine's holding as 60.05% on p. 104 and 60.06% on p. 160; I use "about 60%" and have not verified which figure is right (UNVERIFIED).

The report says no expert's report was legally required, so Domain's directors commissioned one from Grant Samuel (p. 105). The fee is fixed at A$325,000 and does not depend on the conclusions or the outcome (p. 100).

### The question the expert answered

The report has to say whether the scheme is in the best interests of shareholders other than CoStar. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 111 splits that into "fair" and "reasonable" for control transactions (p. 105). The expert's fairness test is a comparison: the scheme is fair if the A$4.43 falls inside its estimated underlying value range for Domain, valued as if 100% of it were available to a willing, informed buyer (p. 106). "Reasonable" looks at other factors, such as the shareholding structure, the chance of a better offer and where the shares would trade without the scheme (p. 106). A fair offer is always reasonable, but a reasonable offer need not be fair (p. 106).

### The result

Grant Samuel valued Domain at A$2,580 to A$2,830 million, which is A$4.06 to A$4.46 per share (p. 131). The table on that page reads, in A$ millions:

| Line | Low | High |
|---|---|---|
| Business operations | 2,775.0 | 3,025.0 |
| Other assets and liabilities | (7.6) | (7.6) |
| Enterprise value | 2,767.4 | 3,017.4 |
| Adjusted net debt | (187.0) | (187.0) |
| Value of equity | 2,580.4 | 2,830.4 |
| Fully diluted shares (millions) | 635.5 | 634.4 |
| Value per share | A$4.06 | A$4.46 |

I checked the arithmetic: 2,775.0 less 7.6 is 2,767.4, less 187.0 is 2,580.4, and 2,580.4 divided by 635.5 is A$4.0604. At the high end 3,025.0 less 7.6 less 187.0 is 2,830.4, and 2,830.4 divided by 634.4 is A$4.4615. The share count is lower at the high end because fewer share rights vest: 3,266,201 at the low end and 2,208,343 at the high end, and the 1,057,858 difference vest only if performance tests at 30 June 2025 are met (footnote 43, p. 131). The A$4.43 offer falls inside the range, so the expert concluded the scheme is fair, and that it is reasonable as a result (p. 157).

The business operations are valued "on a control basis" (p. 134). The expert says the range includes a premium for control and exceeds the price at which it would expect Domain shares to trade without a change of control proposal, and that shares in a listed company normally trade at a 15% to 25% discount to underlying value, though not always (p. 131).

### The methods it considered and the two it used

The report lists four methods: capitalising earnings or cash flows, discounting projected cash flows, industry rules of thumb, and an orderly realisation of assets (p. 131). It used the first two. It found no rule of thumb that applies to Domain, and it judged a realisation of assets inappropriate because Domain is a going concern (p. 134). The earnings approach uses EBITDA and EBITA multiples, and the expert places particular reliance on the multiples implied by its range compared with those of listed peers and past transactions (p. 132).

It says both methods have limits. The DCF depends on a wide range of defensible assumptions, in particular Residential listing volumes and yield, employee numbers and expense growth, EBITDA margin and capital expenditure, so the NPVs vary widely (p. 135). The multiples are hard to read because no Australian digital property marketplace has been sold, software that is developed in-house changes EBITDA against EBITA, and a cyclical sector's multiples depend on the point in the cycle (p. 135).

### How it reached the range

The report says the range for business operations is "a judgement" and "not a mechanical process", reached by an iterative process that tries to fit both the DCF scenarios and the market evidence on multiples. It gave both methods weight, used neither as a cross-check, and says the approach cannot be reduced to a weighting calculation (p. 135).

- DCF: eight scenarios, A to H, at a discount rate of 9.5% to 10.5%. The NPVs are shown only as a bar chart (p. 144) and the report publishes no scenario NPV as a number. The selected range of A$2,775 to A$3,025 million is "a subjective balancing of the scenarios" (p. 144).
- Multiples: the range implies 21.2x to 23.1x FY24 adjusted EBITDA, 19.9x to 21.7x FY25 broker-median EBITDA and 18.2x to 19.9x FY26 broker-median EBITDA, and 28.8x to 31.3x, 25.5x to 27.8x and 23.3x to 25.4x for EBITA (p. 153). The expert places more weight on the FY26 multiples and summarises them as 18 to 20 times EBITDA and 23 to 25 times EBITA (p. 153). I recomputed these from the earnings table on p. 136 and they agree to one decimal place.

### The earnings behind the multiples

The expert deducts from reported EBITDA and EBITA a proxy for the share of earnings that belongs to agents who own equity in Domain subsidiaries. The proxy is the dividend paid to them divided by one minus the 30% tax rate, for example A$4.3 million divided by 0.70 is A$6.1 million for FY24 (p. 136, footnote 44). The result, in A$ millions:

| | FY24 actual | FY25 broker median | FY26 broker median |
|---|---|---|---|
| EBITDA | 137.1 | 146.3 | 160.0 |
| Less non-controlling interests | (6.1) | (6.9) | (7.8) |
| Adjusted EBITDA | 131.0 | 139.4 | 152.2 |
| EBITA | 102.6 | 115.8 | 127.0 |
| Less non-controlling interests | (6.1) | (6.9) | (7.8) |
| Adjusted EBITA | 96.5 | 108.9 | 119.2 |

The forecasts here are the median of broker forecasts, not management's. The report says they are close enough to Domain's own FY25 forecast and FY26 projection to be useful, but it does not publish management's forecasts or the five-year plan (pp. 108 and 136).

### Other points that fix what the number means

- Valuation date: 31 December 2024. It starts from the balance sheet at that date and the DCF counts cash flows from 1 January 2025 (p. 135).
- The valuation is cum dividend: it is struck before the special dividend of up to 8.8 cents that would be paid if the scheme proceeds (p. 135).
- Domain is valued as one business, not as Listings plus Ancillary Services, because it reports earnings only for the group, not for each part (p. 136).
- Buyer cost savings: the value includes the saving on listed company costs that any buyer would make (A$4.0 million a year, p. 143) and excludes cost savings that only CoStar could make (p. 137).
- The business operations value is already net of the agents' equity interests and of corporate costs (footnote 42, p. 131 and p. 137).

### What the report does not disclose

Management's FY25 forecast, FY26 projection and five-year plan are not published (p. 108). The report gives no cash flow table, no figure for the terminal value as a share of value (it says only that this share is normally high, p. 138), and no NPV for any scenario as a number (pp. 137 to 145). Where I need those figures I will have to estimate them and label them as my own.

### What the scenarios were used for

The NPVs for each scenario at 9.5% to 10.5% appear only as a bar chart on p. 144, so I have no scenario NPV as a printed number. Grant Samuel says the weight given to each scenario was "subjective and not capable of being expressed in percentage terms" (p. 144). It treats Scenario A (10% yield growth, Domain's record since listing) as a realistic starting point and Scenario B (12%) as possible but aspirational (p. 144). Changes to Ancillary Services revenue, marketing and capital expenditure (Scenarios E, F and G) have minimal effect, and only Scenario H moves the NPV materially (p. 144). The range of NPVs across the scenarios is wider than the A$2,775 to A$3,025 million it selected, and the chosen range sits towards the upper end of most of the NPV outcomes. The expert says that position implicitly allows for the strategic value of being the second participant in a two-participant market, and that the range was also set to fit the multiples evidence (p. 145).

## Summary of the expert's DCF assumptions

Every figure below comes from the table in the previous section, which gives its page.

**Growth.** Grant Samuel holds the volume of new "for sale" listings flat and puts all the growth in Residential yield, which is price and the adoption of premium listing products. Its base case, Scenario A, grows yield by 10% a year, the average since Domain listed in November 2017. That takes total revenue to over A$990 million in FY35, just over 9% a year, with Residential revenue at about A$740 million. Scenario B uses 12% a year and Scenario C uses 9% a year.

**Margins.** Operating expenses grow by about 7% a year, with staff costs growing about 7.5% a year to FY30 and about 4% a year after that. Revenue grows faster than costs, so the EBITDA margin rises from 35.8% in 1HY25 to 41% by FY30 and just below 48% by FY35. The expert accepts that this is above Domain's history and points to REA Group's 63% to 65% as evidence that higher margins are possible in the sector, while saying it would be unrealistic for Domain to match REA Group. EBITDA grows about 13.5% a year to FY30 and then slows to the terminal growth rate.

**Capital expenditure and working capital.** Capital expenditure is almost 9.5% of revenue over the period and 9% in the long term, and depreciation and other amortisation roughly equal it after the early years. Working capital is 2.5% of revenue. Dividends to the agents who own equity in Domain subsidiaries are 1.6% of the prior year's Residential and Media, Developers and Commercial revenue.

**Tax.** The corporate tax rate is 30%.

**Terminal value.** Cash flows run from 1 January 2025 to 30 June 2035. The terminal value at 30 June 2035 capitalises net after-tax cash flows with a perpetual growth rate of 4%, which the expert bases on long-term inflation of 2.5% a year plus real EBITDA growth. The discount rate is 9.5% to 10.5%. The report does not say how much of the value comes from the terminal value, only that the share is normally high (p. 138).

**Corporate costs.** About A$30 million a year of unallocated costs stay in the model, reduced by a A$4.0 million a year saving that any buyer could make on listed company costs.

**What drives the result.** The expert says the other scenarios (Ancillary Services revenue, marketing spend, capital expenditure) have minimal effect on the NPVs and that only a change in the pricing model (Scenario H) moves them materially, besides the yield growth scenarios (p. 144). The 4% terminal growth rate is built on 2.5% inflation plus real growth in EBITDA (p. 142); the booklet gives no figure for the real growth, and 1.5 percentage points is my own subtraction of 2.5% from 4%. I have not judged either assumption here; they are inputs I will argue in the valuation phase.

## The discount rate (booklet pp. 138 to 140)

### What the expert says about the method

Grant Samuel uses the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) for the cost of equity. It calls CAPM "probably the most widely accepted and used methodology" and says more elaborate multi-factor models have not been widely accepted or used in practice (p. 138). It also says the cost of equity "is not a precise or provable number", that the inputs rest on historical data as a proxy for forward-looking estimates, and that the result should be "a broad guide rather than an absolute truth" (pp. 138 and 139). It adds that strict CAPM in recent years gave unrealistically low results because government bond rates were very low, and that this problem has subsided as bond rates have returned towards historical averages (p. 139).

### The cost of equity

The cost of equity range is 10.3% to 10.9% (p. 139). It is the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium:

| Input | Value | Basis | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| Risk-free rate | 4.3% | 10-year Commonwealth Government bond rate as at 30 May 2025 | 139 |
| Market risk premium | 6% | A standard rate Grant Samuel adopts; analysts typically use 5% to 7% | 139 |
| Beta | 1.0 to 1.1 | Judgement, explained below | 139 and 140 |

At a beta of 1.0 the cost of equity is 4.3% plus 6% = 10.3%, and at 1.1 it is 4.3% plus 6.6% = 10.9%, which matches the printed range.

### The beta evidence

Grant Samuel says a reliable beta for Domain is hard to determine, because the estimates differ by source and period (p. 139):

| Source | Domain | Page |
|---|---|---|
| SIRCA (48 months, quarterly) | Between about 1.4 and 2.1 over four years; 1.4 at 31 December 2024 and steady at about 1.4 for nine months | 139 |
| MSCI Barra (60 months, to 30 April 2025) | Historical about 0.4; predicted 0.8 to 0.9 | 139 |
| Bloomberg adjusted (to 30 May 2025) | Four-year 0.7 to 1.1; two-year at or below 0.5 | 139 |

For peers (pp. 139 and 140):

- REA Group, the closest Australian peer: SIRCA 1.2, Barra 0.4 (predicted 0.8), Bloomberg 1.1 to 1.3 over four years and 0.9 to 1.3 over two years. Grant Samuel notes that these include the effect of REA's withdrawn offer for Rightmove in September 2024, which will have distorted its share price (pp. 139 and 140).
- CAR Group and SEEK: SIRCA 1.4 to 1.7, with lower Barra and Bloomberg betas that are generally still at or above 1. The expert says they are not particularly useful because they operate in auto and employment markets that are arguably less cyclical than property, and the report adds that CAR Group made large acquisitions to expand its global footprint and SEEK tried to expand by acquisition, which would have made their share prices more volatile (p. 140).
- Global digital property marketplaces, excluding outliers: Bloomberg four-year betas 0.9 to 1.4 (median 1.0), Bloomberg two-year betas 0.3 to 1.3 (median 0.8), Barra predicted betas 0.8 to 1.1 (median 0.8) (p. 140).

### Why it chose 1.0 to 1.1

The expert's reasons, as I read them (pp. 139 and 140):

1. It would intuitively expect a beta above 1.0 for a cyclical business whose activity is correlated with the market (p. 140).
2. It does not accept the high estimates (SIRCA 1.4 or more) as forward-looking. Domain's shares outperformed after the COVID-19 pandemic, and it argues betas measured over a shorter period that excludes 2021 are more relevant. The fall in Domain's SIRCA beta over time and the lower two-year Bloomberg betas support that view (p. 139).
3. Higher peer betas reflect one-off events such as the pandemic and large acquisitions, so they get less weight in an expected beta (p. 140).

It concludes that 1.0 to 1.1 "is a reasonable estimate of the appropriate beta" (p. 140). The report calls the cost of capital "fundamentally a matter of judgement, not merely a calculation" (p. 139). The printed beta evidence for Domain and its peers runs from about 0.3 to 2.1, so 1.0 to 1.1 is not the output of any one source (my observation).

### From cost of equity to WACC

| Input | Value | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-tax cost of debt | 5.5%, a 1.2 percentage point margin over the risk-free rate, based on expected pricing for a property marketplace and not Domain's existing facilities, with an allowance for establishment costs and liquidity | 140 |
| Tax rate | 30% | 140 |
| Gearing | 85% to 95% equity and 5% to 15% debt, from the gearing of comparable listed companies over four years, not Domain's own | 140 |
| WACC | 9.3% to 10.5% | 140 |

The report says a WACC range of 9.3% to 10.5% results, and that it adopts 9.5% to 10.5% for the DCF (p. 140). It says it considers a discount rate in line with the calculated WACC appropriate and does not say why the adopted range begins at 9.5% and not 9.3%. It notes that this range is higher than the broker analysts' discount rates of 8.4% to 9.0% (median 8.5%), and explains the difference by the brokers using a lower risk-free rate or a lower market risk premium (p. 140).

My recomputation, with the after-tax cost of debt at 5.5% times (1 minus 30%), which is 3.85%: 85% equity at a 10.3% cost of equity gives 0.85 times 10.3% plus 0.15 times 3.85%, which is 9.333%, and 95% equity at 10.9% gives 0.95 times 10.9% plus 0.05 times 3.85%, which is 10.548%. Those match the printed 9.3% to 10.5% at one decimal place. The report does not say which weight goes with which cost of equity, so I chose the combinations that give the lowest and highest values (my arithmetic, not a statement in the booklet).

### Why Zillow and CoStar are out

The report gives one reason for both. Their historical and forecast EBITDA multiples are at or above 75 times, so they do not give meaningful benchmarks, because the market expects large future earnings growth (p. 152). It then describes each business. Zillow is moving from a home-buying model, which it discontinued in 2021, to a "housing super app". CoStar has been building Homes.com since 2021 to challenge Zillow, and the report says its heavy marketing and content investment has reduced its 2024 and forecast 2025 earnings (p. 152).

### From multiples to the range

The range of A$2,775 to A$3,025 million implies 18.2 to 19.9 times FY26 adjusted EBITDA and 23.3 to 25.4 times FY26 adjusted EBITA (p. 153). The expert puts more weight on FY26 because FY25 is already a historical year and its FY25 forecast reflects actual results for the 11 months to 31 May 2025 (p. 153). Against listed peers, the implied multiples are well below REA (33 times forecast EBITDA, 38 times EBITA), Hemnet, Baltic and Vend (27 to 32 times forecast EBITDA), and comparable to CAR Group, SEEK, Scout24 and Rightmove (about 16 to 23 times forecast EBITDA) (p. 154). The report lists reasons that Domain deserves a high absolute multiple (strategic position as number two in a two-participant market, brand, track record, growth opportunities, the Nine relationship, and the Australian market) and reasons that constrain it (smaller scale, being a "price follower", lower margins, cyclicality and mixed results in Ancillary Services), and concludes that the implied multiples "reflect a reasonable balance" (pp. 154 and 155).

## The control premium and the fairness conclusion (booklet pp. 157 to 160)

### The conclusion

Grant Samuel concludes the scheme is fair and reasonable (p. 157). The A$4.43 falls inside its range of A$4.06 to A$4.46 per share and "very close to the top end", which the expert says probably reflects Domain's strategic value as the second participant in a two-participant market and a shareholding structure in which CoStar needed Nine's support (p. 157). It adds that Nine, as an arm's length party, has no apparent reason to accept less than fair value, and that this reinforces fairness (p. 157). The reasoning is that, as the scheme is fair, it is also reasonable, and it then lists further factors (p. 157).

For scale, A$4.43 sits A$0.37 above the bottom of the range and A$0.03 below the top, which is 92.5% of the way up the range (my arithmetic: 0.37 divided by 0.40). It is A$0.17, or 4.0%, above the A$4.26 midpoint I use later for the gap bridge.

### The premium the offer represents

The A$4.43 is a 42% premium to the last close before the original proposal, A$3.12 on 20 February 2025, and the premium rises to about 50% to 60% against the one-month to six-month averages (p. 157). The report's table, with my recomputation from the printed prices:

A worked example from the booklet's figures: the premium over the close is (4.43 minus 3.12) divided by 3.12, which is 1.31 divided by 3.12, or 42.0%. The original A$4.20 proposal was a 34.6% premium on the same close, so the A$0.23 increase added 7.4 percentage points (p. 159; the 7.4 is my subtraction of 34.6% from 42.0%).

### How the expert treats the premium

- It says the premiums are "well above" the 20% to 35% typically associated with Australian takeovers, and that premiums are "an outcome not a determinant of value" and vary widely, with a substantial proportion of deals outside the 20% to 35% range (pp. 133 and 158).
- It considers recent prices a more appropriate base than the one, three and six month VWAPs, because those reflect lower prices earlier in the period, and the share price rose after the 1HY25 results on 13 February 2025 (p. 158).
- Domain's closing price over the three years before the proposal ranged from A$2.44 to A$4.25 and never reached A$4.43 (p. 158). It last traded above A$4.43 between November 2020 and February 2022. The intraday high was A$6.03 on 5 November 2021, unadjusted for the April 2022 entitlement offer, which the expert attributes to "market (over) exuberance" (p. 158).
- It sees no event that artificially affected the price before the proposal (p. 158).
- It explains a premium above 35% by strategic value, since CoStar has no other way into the Australian property marketplace sector at scale, and by Nine's position, because CoStar needed Nine's support and a higher price was negotiated after the A$4.20 proposal (p. 159). The original proposal's premiums, 33.7% to 51.4%, were "more consistent" with the typical Australian level, at the high end (p. 159).

### The premium inside the valuation

The expert's A$4.06 to A$4.46 range "includes a premium for control" (p. 131), and shares in a listed company normally trade at a 15% to 25% discount to underlying value (p. 131). It does not publish a premium percentage for its own range. A worked example from those two figures: if underlying value is 100, shares at a 15% discount trade at 85 and a buyer paying 100 pays a premium of 100 divided by 85 minus 1, which is 17.6%; at a 25% discount the premium is 100 divided by 75 minus 1, which is 33.3% (my arithmetic). The report gives 20% to 35% as the typical premium (p. 133), so the two ranges overlap (my observation).

### Reasonableness

The report's other reasons the scheme is reasonable (pp. 160 and 161):

- Without the scheme, it expects Domain shares to trade materially below A$4.43, and sees no reason they would trade materially above the A$3.00 to A$3.20 range before the proposal (p. 160).
- Liquidity would fall. CoStar holds 16.96% and Nine about 60%, leaving a free float of about 23% and a possible overhang from CoStar's stake (p. 160).
- No third party could succeed without Nine (p. 160). CoStar has matching rights and has said A$4.43 is its best and final price in the absence of a competing proposal, and the deed lets Domain respond to an unsolicited superior proposal (pp. 160 and 161).
- No superior proposal had been received since 21 February 2025, and the scheme meeting was set for 4 August 2025 (p. 161).
- If the scheme fails, Domain expects to bear costs of about A$3.6 million (0.6 cents a share) and could owe CoStar a A$28.1 million break fee (p. 161).
- Part of the A$4.43 may be a franked special dividend of up to 8.8 cents, which reduces the cash consideration, and the expert does not value franking credits in the consideration (p. 161).
